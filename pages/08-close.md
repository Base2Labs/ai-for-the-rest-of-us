# It's not that it doesn't work

- MIT: roughly **95%** of corporate generative-AI pilots deliver no measurable return.
- The ones that worked aimed at **growth**, not at cutting headcount — 2–3× more likely, per McKinsey.
- The technology is real. Most attempts to apply it are still bad.
- **But the failure I want to end on isn't organisational. It's the one that happens to you.**

# What it does to you

- **39%** of workers say AI is making them **less intelligent**. Half say they rely on it too much.
- **43%** have shipped AI output they *suspected* was wrong.
- **60%** feel **pressured** to use it — which is the mechanism, not a detail.
- MIT Media Lab measured the brain doing it: the more the assistant helped, the weaker the neural engagement. They call it **cognitive debt**.

<!--
Now the personal cost, and I want to be careful to give you real numbers rather than a vibe.

GoTo surveyed two and a half thousand employees this year. Thirty-nine percent said they think AI is making them less intelligent. Half said they rely on it too much. These are not critics — these are people using it daily, reporting on themselves.

Forty-three percent said they had used AI output they suspected was low quality or contained errors. Sit on that word: suspected. Not "failed to notice". They had the doubt, and shipped it anyway.

So why would somebody do that? The same survey answers it. Sixty percent said they feel pressured to use AI. That is the mechanism. It is not stupidity and it is not laziness — it is an ordinary person at four o'clock on a Thursday with too much to do and a tool that will make the doubt go away.

And it shows up in instruments, not just surveys. MIT's Media Lab ran EEG on people writing essays with and without an assistant, and found brain connectivity scaled down with the amount of help — the group using the model showed the weakest engagement, and stayed behind over four months. They gave it a good name: cognitive debt. You get the essay now and pay for it later, in understanding you never built.

Remember the seventeen percent from earlier — that is the same finding from a different angle. This is not one study you can wave away.

# "AI could have done it in five minutes"

- ***Forevergreen*** — an Oscar-nominated animated short. **200+ artists. Five years. Donated free time.**
- A head teacher showed it to his class. One boy's response: *"AI could have done it in five minutes."*
- Not a sneer. The teacher's words: **"baffled practicality."** He genuinely couldn't see the point.
- Nobody in that room was wrong about the facts. That's what makes it worth worrying about.

# Three kinds of people

Brooks' frame: what separates people now isn't intelligence. It's their **relationship to mental effort**.

1. **Cognitive misers** — find thinking unpleasant. AI is a permission slip to stop.
2. **Mental marathoners** — insist on running it anyway, the way people run 26.2 miles in a world with cars.
3. **Everyone else, in the middle** — and which way they drift decides this.

<!--
So if that is where it ends up, the question is what decides whether you get there. And Brooks' answer is the reason I find him more useful than the usual jobs conversation.

At the other end, the ones who insist on doing the thinking anyway. Brooks calls them mental marathoners, and the analogy is exact — the car is a perfectly good way to travel 26.2 miles. There is no practical reason for any person to run it. Some people do, and not because they are confused about cars.

Most of us are in the middle, and drift depending on the day and how tired we are. That is the honest position, and it is where that forty-three percent number comes from — those are not bad people, those are ordinary people at four o'clock on a Thursday.

Brooks' worry is that the middle splits. Minds that atrophy and minds that compound, pulling apart. What decides it is not talent. It is a hundred small choices about whether to do the hard part — and the sixty percent who feel pressured are having those choices made for them by their calendar.

Which sounds like bad news, and is where I would have stopped if the last slide did not exist.

# Stretching, not atrophying

Use it for what you **can't** do — not just what you already can.

Automating today's work makes you smaller.<br/>Reaching past your grasp makes you larger.

The good news: appetite for hard thinking is **not fixed**. It responds to context.

Which means it's a choice, repeatedly — and you can build the conditions for it.
<!--
Here is the turn, and it is the whole reason this is a close rather than a warning.

Use it for what you cannot do, rather than only for what you can. Point it at what you already do and you get the same output faster, and a slightly smaller version of yourself each time. Point it at something previously out of reach and you get stretched — you end up understanding things you could not have understood alone.

Same tool. Opposite outcomes. The difference is entirely in what you asked of it.

And here is the genuinely hopeful part, which is why this is a close and not a warning. Brooks is clear that appetite for hard thinking is not a fixed trait. It is extremely sensitive to context. It can be cultivated, or it can be eroded — by pressure, by deadlines, by tools that make it a little too easy to skip the part where you think.

Which means this is not a personality test you already passed or failed. It is a choice, made repeatedly, in small moments, and you can build the conditions that make it easier.
