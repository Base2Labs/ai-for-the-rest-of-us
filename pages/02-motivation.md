# Four quadrants of skill

1. **Syntax** — knowing the incantation.
2. **Algorithms** — the logic and the maths.
3. **Decomposition** — turning "we need something like this" into something buildable.
4. **Systems thinking** — how the parts interact, and what happens when one fails.

The first two got very cheap, very fast. The last two didn't move at all — and **value flows to scarcity.**

<!--
Here is the frame that makes the rest of the talk make sense, and it is not really about programming.

Split the skill into four parts. Knowing the vocabulary and the incantations. Working out the logic. Taking something vague that a human wants and turning it into something precise enough to build. And understanding how all the pieces interact, so it does not collapse when one of them fails.

AI flattened the first two almost completely. It has barely touched the last two.

That is the important shape, and notice it is not "AI is good at easy things." Some of what it does is genuinely hard. It is that the machine got very good at the parts with patterns in them, and stayed weak at the parts requiring you to know what is actually wanted and what happens when reality intrudes.

Now the economics. Value flows to whatever is scarce. For thirty years the scarce thing was people who could make a computer do what they wanted. That is no longer scarce. What is left — deciding what should exist, and keeping it alive — just became disproportionately valuable.

Which brings us to the question everyone in this room is actually here to ask.

# The bottleneck moved — it didn't disappear

- Say it out loud: *is this coming for my job too?*
- The honest answer: the work relocated. It did not evaporate.
- **Upstream** — deciding what is worth building, and being right about it.
- **Downstream** — integrating, operating, and keeping it alive when it breaks.
- The measured effect on people's days wasn't emptiness. It was intensity.

<!--

I am not going to reassure you, because reassurance from someone whose field it hit first is worth nothing. Here is what I can actually tell you.

The bottleneck moved. Building things was the constraint for decades — never enough people who could do it. That constraint is largely gone, and when a constraint goes, the pressure does not vanish, it relocates to whatever is now tightest. It went upstream, to deciding what is worth building and being right about it. And downstream, to integrating and running and fixing the much larger volume of stuff now being produced.

And the data on this is genuinely surprising. ActiveTrack studied over ten thousand workers after AI adoption. Time in email and chat more than doubled. Business software use went up ninety-four percent. Focused, uninterrupted work fell nine percent. Berkeley researchers found people took back tasks they had previously delegated away.

Nobody's day got emptier. Everybody's day got faster and more fragmented. There is already a name going around for how that feels — AI brain fry.

So the real risk is not that there is nothing left for you to do. It is what happens to you while you are doing it. That is the question I want to end on in about an hour, and I think there is a good answer to it.

# This already left software

- Paramedics, glaciologists, kayakers, restaurant operators, sales leaders — building their own tools.
- A bot that phones restaurants, books the table, handles the invite, declares the allergies.
- Product photography without the photoshoot. Meeting minutes returning **four hours a day** to one assistant.
- The story isn't how software gets built. It's *what* gets built, and *who* builds it.
